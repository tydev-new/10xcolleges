> **STUDENT DRAFT**

The bike stand outside the public library smelled of chain grease and damp concrete. On Tuesday afternoons, commuters would pull up with stripped gears and squealing brakes.

I spent most of junior year fixing those drivetrains with an adjustable wrench and a bucket of degreaser. When a commuter named Arthur brought in a 1980s ten-speed with a cracked derailleur cage, the local bike shop told him it was trash. I took it apart on the asphalt, measured the cage tolerances with calipers, and bent a scrap aluminum plate to clamp the pulley wheels back in alignment. When he pedaled away with smooth shifts, I realized that mechanical engineering wasn't about pristine blueprints — it was about taking what was broken and making it work with your hands.
